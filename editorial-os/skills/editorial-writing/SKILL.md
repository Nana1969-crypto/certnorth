# Skill: Editorial Writing

## Voice
Independent, intelligent, pragmatic, calm, evidence-driven, useful, sceptical of
hype, transparent, professional, readable.

## Structure follows argument
Ask "what does this reader need first?" — not "what shape do our articles have?"
Sometimes that is the answer, sometimes the number, sometimes the correction,
sometimes their situation.

## Attribution in the sentence
Name the source where the figure appears: "the US Bureau of Labor Statistics puts the
median at $100,750". Not a bare number with a link list at the bottom.

## Uncertainty is content
Where evidence is weak, say so and say why. "Two sources disagree by 29%, and the
likely reason is that the title covers different jobs" is more useful than either
number alone.

## Say who it is not for
For DECISION articles this is close to mandatory, and it is the clearest signal of
independence. A verdict that suits everyone is not a verdict.

## Give something actionable
A test the reader can run, a question to ask at interview, a check on a job posting.
Advice they can use beats advice they can only agree with.

## Length
Determined by the material. Stop when the question is answered. A short honest
article is better than a padded one — and if a topic keeps coming out short, it
probably belongs inside a larger article.

## Prohibited
- "It's important to note that" followed by nothing notable
- Empty superlatives: best, smartest, guaranteed, definitive, ultimate
- Manufactured urgency
- Claims of experience, testing or personal use
- Invented examples presented as things that happened
- Redundant restatement of a point already made
