## A. Page mission

> **This page exists to convert "is the PMP worth it?" into a question the reader can
> actually answer about themselves, using evidence CertNorth has already established
> elsewhere — and to be honest that the evidence supports one kind of value and not
> another.**

### What question it answers

*"Given where I am, what I do, and where I work — should I pursue the PMP, and if not now,
then when?"*

### What the reader needs before deciding

Whether they can apply at all · what the evidence does and does not show about pay ·
what it costs to earn **and keep** · whether their own market asks for it · what else the
same money and months could buy.

### What belongs here

The **verdict and the reasoning that produces it**. The synthesis across CertNorth's own
evidence. The conditions under which the answer flips. A test the reader can run.

### What deliberately stays in supporting articles

| Subject | Stays with |
|---|---|
| Fee breakdown, membership economics, ten-year framing | `pmp-cost` |
| The 36/60-month pathways and the 35 contact hours | `pmp-eligibility` |
| The salary survey's methodology and confounding | `does-pmp-increase-salary` |
| Why no pass rate exists | `pmp-pass-rate` |
| PDU mechanics and renewal mechanics | `pmp-renewal-pdus` |
| The CAPM ladder | `pmp-vs-capm` |
| Whether *any* certification is needed | `do-you-need-certification-project-manager` |
| Exam format and the July 2026 domain shift | `how-to-prepare-for-pmp-exam` |

### Different from `pmp-cost` how

`pmp-cost` answers **"what will I spend?"** and is organised around money and uncertainty.
This page answers **"should I spend it?"** and is organised around the reader's decision
sequence. `pmp-cost` refuses to give a number; this page refuses to give a universal yes or
no. Neither shape may be reused for the other.

### Different from the other seven `is-X-worth-it` pages how

Those render a verdict through pros, cons and a bottom line — three headings this article
currently shares verbatim with all seven. This one **stops rendering a verdict and starts
issuing a test.** The reader leaves with a procedure and a set of conditions, not with our
opinion.

## B. Recommended thesis

Three were developed. The recommendation is **Thesis 1**.

### Thesis 1 — *The PMP buys access, not income* ✅ RECOMMENDED

> The available evidence supports the PMP as an **access credential** — something that gets
> an application considered, satisfies a filter, or unlocks a grade — and does **not**
> establish that it raises pay by itself. So the useful question is not "is it worth it?"
> but **"is there a door in my market that this opens and that is currently closed to
> me?"** — which the reader can check directly, today.

**Evidence supporting it**

| Evidence | Source | Type |
|---|---|---|
| The salary premium compares PMP holders (who must have 36–60 months' experience) against a group with no experience floor | `does-pmp-increase-salary` | CertNorth synthesis of a source claim |
| PMI's own survey reports the premium as 16%, 23%, and 17–33% across editions — a quantity that moves that much is noisy | `does-pmp-increase-salary` | Source claim + CertNorth synthesis |
| The plausible mechanisms named are HR filters, postings naming the credential, and formal grade prerequisites — **all access mechanisms** | `does-pmp-increase-salary` | CertNorth synthesis |
| BLS: certification is **not required** for the occupation; it "may be beneficial" and "demonstrates competency to employers" | `do-you-need-certification-project-manager` | Established fact (source wording) |
| Geography decides recognition — PRINCE2 is expected where the PMP is not | `pmp-vs-prince2` | CertNorth synthesis |

**What would falsify or weaken it**

A study controlling for experience that still found a premium would move this from "not
established" to "supported". Employers ceasing to name the credential in postings would
weaken the access claim itself. Neither is currently in evidence.

**Why it is original value**

It reframes the question from one nobody can answer for the reader (will it pay?) into one
the reader can answer in an afternoon (does my market ask for it?). **No training provider
can publish this framing** — their business needs the premium to be causal.

### Thesis 2 — *Eligibility is the real gate*

> By the time you can apply, you have three to five years of project experience — and
> anyone with that has already learned whether their market wants the credential.

**Support:** the eligibility floor is real and consistent site-wide.
**Weakness:** it is close to fatalistic and gives the reader less to do. It is a *component*
of Thesis 1 (gate 1), not a thesis in its own right.

### Thesis 3 — *The answer is entirely local*

> The PMP's value is set by your market, employer and target grade; a global verdict is not
> possible.

**Support:** BLS (not required nationally), the geographic split, grade prerequisites.
**Weakness:** true but nearly contentless on its own — "it depends" is not an article.
**Absorbed into Thesis 1** as gate 2.

## C. Evidence chain

For each block: **established fact** · **source claim** · **CertNorth synthesis** ·
**uncertainty**.

### A. Is the PMP required to become a project manager?

- **Established fact:** BLS states certification is not a requirement for the occupation;
  its wording is that certification "may be beneficial" and "demonstrates competency to
  employers and clients." BLS also states entry typically requires a bachelor's degree.
- **Source claim:** BLS projects ~78,200 openings a year and 6% growth 2024–2034.
- **CertNorth synthesis:** in a field hiring at that rate, employers cannot filter everyone
  out on certification — the argument `do-you-need-certification-project-manager` already
  makes.
- **Uncertainty:** BLS speaks about **certification generically, not the PMP**, and about
  the **occupation, not individual employers**. It cannot settle whether a specific employer
  wants a specific credential. **See §3.**

### B. Does the PMP correlate with higher salary?

- **Source claim:** PMI's *Earning Power* survey — 30,000+ respondents, 40 countries,
  stratified sampling, disclosed methodology — reports a PMP premium of 16%, 23%, and
  17–33% depending on edition and country.
- **Established fact:** to hold a PMP you must already have 36–60 months of documented
  project experience.
- **CertNorth synthesis:** the comparison group has **no experience floor**, so it includes
  first-role and occasional project workers. Those people would earn less than the certified
  group *even if the PMP did not exist*. Additional confounds: PMI's own community as the
  sampling frame, self-reported pay, and selection effects.
- **Uncertainty:** the survey **cannot establish causation**, and no experience-controlled
  study is available. **The absence of such a study is not evidence that the premium is
  zero.** The honest position: certificate contributes something real, experience
  contributes a great deal more, and the size of the certificate's contribution is unknown.

### C. What can we know about pass rates?

- **Established fact:** PMI has not published a pass rate for roughly two decades. The
  circulating "61%" is a *passing score* retired around 2006. PMI scores psychometrically
  and reports performance bands.
- **CertNorth synthesis:** every probability-weighted "expected cost of the PMP" in
  circulation rests on a number that does not exist.
- **Uncertainty:** permanent and unresolvable. **This is a known unknown, not a gap to fill.**

### D. What does it cost beyond the exam?

- **Established fact (verified at PMI):** exam $405 member / $655 non-member; membership
  $139/year.
- **CertNorth calculation:** joining is $111 cheaper for a single first attempt.
- **Established fact:** required 35 contact hours, priced by third parties; PMI publishes no
  single price for them.
- **Uncertainty:** renewal fee not established as a universal figure; retake fee not
  established; regional pricing varies. `pmp-cost` deliberately publishes no long-term total.

### E. What does maintaining it require?

- **Established fact (verified in the CCR Handbook):** 60 PDUs per three-year cycle; at
  least 35 from Education; at most 25 from Giving Back; **at most 8 from Work as a
  Practitioner.**
- **CertNorth synthesis:** holding the job contributes at most 8 of 60 — **employment does
  not maintain the credential.** At least 35 hours per cycle of deliberate learning, for as
  long as you hold it.
- **Uncertainty:** the renewal fee amount.

### F. What alternatives exist?

- **Established fact:** CAPM requires no experience — $225 / $300, 23 contact hours.
  PSM I has no prerequisites and never expires.
- **CertNorth synthesis:** for a targeted agile role a Scrum credential is cheaper and more
  direct; for a career-changer the PMP is not available at all; PRINCE2 is expected in some
  geographies.
- **Uncertainty:** none material.

## D. Decision framework — four gates

The reader passes through these **in order**. Each can stop them, and stopping is a real
outcome rather than a failure.

| Gate | The question | If the answer is no |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Eligibility** | Can you document 36 or 60 months of project experience? | The question is moot. → `pmp-eligibility`, and the CAPM route |
| **2. Access** | Does your market actually ask for it — postings, filters, promotion grades? | The strongest argument for it does not apply to you. Revisit when you change employer or market |
| **3. Cost** | Can you carry the exam, the required education, and **the maintenance** — 60 PDUs per cycle, at least 35 of them deliberate learning, indefinitely? | The recurring commitment is the part people underestimate |
| **4. Opportunity** | What else would those months and that money buy — a Scrum credential, a domain qualification, a delivered project you can describe? | If the alternative is more useful in your market, take it |

**Variables the framework must handle:** career stage · current responsibilities · target
role · employer expectations · geographic market · eligibility · financial cost · time cost
· maintenance burden · opportunity cost · alternatives · likelihood of actually using it.

**The framework produces conditions, not a verdict.** It must be possible for a reader to
finish the article and conclude "not for me", "not yet", or "yes, and here is why" — all
three being fully supported outcomes.

## §3. The BLS issue, resolved precisely

**What the source establishes**, per the wording the site already quotes:

| Proposition | Does BLS support it? |
|---|---|
| Certification is **not a requirement** for the occupation | **Yes** — this is the source's own wording |
| Certification "may be beneficial" and "demonstrates competency to employers and clients" | **Yes** — quoted wording, deliberately non-quantified |
| Entry to the occupation typically requires a bachelor's degree | **Yes** |
| The field is growing — ~78,200 openings/yr, 6% growth 2024–2034 | **Yes** |
| **"Project managers don't need certification"** as career advice | **No** — that overreads an occupational statement into individual guidance |
| **Employers don't value the PMP** | **No** — BLS says the opposite direction, weakly: it *may be beneficial* |
| Anything about **the PMP specifically** | **No** — BLS speaks about certification generically |
| Anything about **differentiation between two candidates** | **No** — outside what the source measures |

**The four distinctions the article must keep apart:**

1. **Occupational requirement** — BLS answers this. Not required.
2. **Employer preference** — BLS gestures at it ("may be beneficial") without measuring it.
   **Only the reader's own market can answer this**, which is why gate 2 exists.
3. **Certification value** — BLS does not measure it. `does-pmp-increase-salary` does, and
   finds it confounded.
4. **Career differentiation** — no source available. **Say so.**

**How it should affect the framework:** BLS moves the PMP out of the "required" column and
into the "differentiator" column — and a differentiator's worth is local by definition.
**That is the evidential bridge to gate 2**, and it is the correct use of a source the
article currently cites and ignores.

## §4. The salary claim, resolved

**The article must not imply causation.** The formulation to use, in substance:

> PMI's own survey reports a premium, and the survey is large and its methodology is
> published. But it compares people who by definition have three to five years of
> experience against a group with no experience floor — so part of the gap is experience,
> not the certificate, and **how much of it is the certificate is not known.**

**Three things this must not become:**

- **not** "the PMP raises your salary by 17%" — the comparison cannot show that;
- **not** "the salary premium is a myth" — that turns absence of evidence into evidence of
  absence, and the survey is real;
- **not** a restatement of `does-pmp-increase-salary` — one paragraph and a link.

**What it becomes instead:** the pivot into the thesis. If the pay effect cannot be
established but the access mechanisms can, then access is the thing to evaluate.

## §5. The price-change claim, resolved

**Claim:** *"PMI adjusted its fees during 2026 and prices also vary by country."*

**Can it be verified?** Not from this environment, and not from any evidence this project
has assembled. The verified figures ($405 / $655) match no before-and-after pair we could
establish. The increase narrative traces to vendor pages the protocol correction already
ruled out as evidence, and those pages proposed figures ($445 / $675) that turned out to be
wrong.

**Recommendation: REMOVE.** Do not soften it, do not attribute it, do not replace it with a
different unverified movement. The regional-variation half of the sentence is separately
supported and may stay.

**`pmp-vs-capm` carries a stronger form:** *"PMI raised PMP exam fees in 2026, so the gap
between the two is now wider than older articles suggest."* That is an unsupported claim
plus an unsupported inference from it.

**Recommendation: correct it in a later, separate task.** It is out of scope here, and
**this diagnostic does not modify it.**

## J. Original-value map

Six things a reader can use:

1. **A four-gate decision sequence** that produces "no", "not yet" or "yes, because" — and
   treats stopping as a valid outcome.
2. **The access-versus-income distinction**, which reframes a question nobody can answer for
   the reader into one they can.
3. **A market test the reader can run today** on job postings in their own market.
4. **The distinction between occupational requirement, employer preference, certification
   value and differentiation** — four things routinely collapsed into one.
5. **The maintenance correction**: employment contributes at most 8 of 60 PDUs, so holding
   the job does not hold the credential.
6. **A stated falsification condition** — what evidence would change CertNorth's answer, and
   when the reader should revisit.

**No fabricated data. No proprietary research. No survey of our own.** Every item is
synthesis, reasoning or a procedure.
